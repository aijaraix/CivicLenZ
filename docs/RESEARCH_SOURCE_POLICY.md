# CivicLenZ Research and Source Policy

## 1. Purpose

CivicLenZ must make it possible for a reasonable reader to understand where a claim came from, how it was interpreted, how current it is, and whether it has been disputed. This policy governs automated collection, human research, AI analysis, publication, correction, and scoring.

## 2. Source hierarchy

### Tier 1 — Primary official records

Preferred for legal, financial, voting, office, and procedural facts:

- Government websites and official directories.
- Legislative journals, roll calls, bill systems, ordinances, executive orders, budgets, minutes, agendas, and public notices.
- Election authorities and certified election results.
- Campaign-finance and financial-disclosure filing systems.
- Court dockets and published judicial records.
- Ethics commissions, inspectors general, auditors, regulators, and enforcement agencies.
- Official public-record responses.

### Tier 2 — Primary statements and materials

Preferred for promises, positions, and direct quotations:

- Official and campaign websites.
- Archived campaign pages.
- Speeches, debates, press conferences, interviews, testimony, advertisements, newsletters, social posts, and podcasts.
- Candidate questionnaires, voter guides, policy papers, and platform PDFs.
- Direct official responses submitted to CivicLenZ.

### Tier 3 — Reputable secondary reporting

Useful for context, discovery, and reporting not available in a primary source:

- Established news organizations.
- Local newspapers and public-interest newsrooms.
- Recognized investigative journalism organizations.
- Wire services and specialist trade publications.

### Tier 4 — Specialist databases and research organizations

Useful when methodology and sourcing are visible:

- Nonpartisan legislative, election, finance, legal, policy, and transparency databases.
- University and research-center datasets.
- Credible nonprofit watchdogs and public-interest organizations.

### Tier 5 — Self-published, partisan, advocacy, or user-submitted material

May be used as evidence of what the publisher said or alleged, but generally should not be treated as independent verification of the underlying fact.

### Tier 6 — Unknown or low-verifiability sources

May be retained for discovery or review but should not support a consequential public claim without corroboration.

## 3. Required evidence metadata

Each evidence item should include:

- Source title.
- Publisher, agency, or account.
- Author or speaker where available.
- Canonical URL.
- Archived URL where available.
- Publication, event, filing, or vote date.
- Retrieval date.
- Exact page, section, bill action, roll-call identifier, filing line, transcript segment, or media timestamp.
- Relevant excerpt or structured record.
- Source type and tier.
- Evidence role: supports, contradicts, contextualizes, or official response.
- Content/document hash when stored.
- Verification and review state.

## 4. Direct quotations

- Preserve exact wording, punctuation, and context as accurately as the source allows.
- Store the full surrounding context or a link/locator sufficient to review it.
- Do not combine separate statements into a single quotation.
- Label paraphrases as summaries, not quotations.
- For audio/video, store timestamp and transcript provenance.
- Correct transcription errors visibly and preserve the earlier version in audit history.

## 5. Campaign promise identification

A statement may be classified as a campaign promise when it is a reasonably clear future commitment by the candidate or campaign. Research must search:

- Campaign homepages.
- Platform and issue pages.
- Archived versions of the campaign site.
- Campaign PDFs and policy plans.
- Voter guides and candidate questionnaires.
- Debates, interviews, speeches, town halls, and advertisements.
- Social posts and newsletters.
- Explicit “I will,” “we will,” “I promise,” “I pledge,” “I will not,” or equivalent commitments.

Avoid classifying general values, aspirations, predictions, or descriptions of existing policy as measurable promises unless the commitment is explicit.

## 6. Allegations, investigations, and findings

CivicLenZ must never collapse these categories:

- Allegation.
- Complaint.
- Inquiry or preliminary review.
- Formal investigation.
- Charge or enforcement action.
- Administrative or judicial finding.
- Settlement without admission.
- Dismissal.
- Acquittal.
- Conviction or final adverse determination.
- Appeal or reversal.

Each record must state the authority, procedural status, date, underlying document, official response, and final disposition when available.

## 7. Conflicting evidence

When credible sources conflict:

- Preserve both claims and evidence.
- Do not silently choose a winner.
- Explain the nature of the conflict.
- Prefer primary records for the fact those records are authoritative about.
- Mark the canonical value as conflicting or under review when resolution is not justified.
- Avoid publishing a numeric score that depends materially on an unresolved conflict unless the methodology defines a transparent uncertainty rule.

## 8. AI use

AI may:

- Extract candidate facts.
- Classify records.
- Summarize source material.
- Match entities.
- Detect possible contradictions.
- Identify possible promises.
- Draft issue-position analysis.
- Suggest score inputs.

AI may not:

- Fabricate missing evidence.
- Present an inference as a sourced fact.
- Infer sensitive personal characteristics.
- Convert allegations into findings.
- Publish high-impact misconduct labels without required review.
- Hide conflicting or incomplete evidence.

Every stored model result should include model/version, prompt or rule version, input evidence IDs, output, confidence, and review state.

## 9. Human review triggers

Human review is required before public publication when a record involves:

- Criminal, ethics, fraud, corruption, harassment, discrimination, or other serious misconduct allegations.
- Identity ambiguity or duplicate merging.
- A disputed quotation.
- A material score change.
- A promise labeled broken, reversed, or deceptively stated.
- A conflict-of-interest determination.
- A claim based only on low-tier sources.
- A correction request from the subject or an affected organization.

## 10. Freshness and rechecking

Source categories should have recheck schedules based on how quickly they change:

- Current office, contact, and social channels: frequent.
- Votes, bills, executive actions, meetings, news, and finance filings: event-driven or daily where feasible.
- Campaign pages and promises: frequent during election periods and archived when changed.
- Biography, education, and career history: periodic.
- Financial disclosures, property, and ethics records: aligned with filing and agency schedules.

Public sections should show last checked, last changed, and stale status.

## 11. Archiving

- Preserve archived copies or references for campaign materials and pages likely to change.
- Retain content hashes and retrieval metadata.
- Do not present an archive as proof that content was live outside the archive timestamp.
- Respect copyright and access restrictions; store metadata and lawful excerpts when full-content storage is not appropriate.

## 12. Corrections and official responses

- Provide a visible correction path.
- Require a source or specific explanation where possible.
- Acknowledge receipt and assign review state.
- Preserve the original publication and all correction decisions.
- Publish material corrections promptly with an explanation.
- Display an official response beside the relevant claim when appropriate.
- Do not remove accurate historical records merely because they are unfavorable.

## 13. Neutrality and consistency

- Apply the same field definitions and evidence rules across parties and ideologies.
- Do not use agreement with a policy position as a civic-performance score.
- Separate position alignment from transparency, responsiveness, integrity, attendance, effectiveness, and promise performance.
- Avoid loaded labels unless the label is a direct quotation or a defined methodology output.

## 14. Publication checklist

Before publishing a consequential claim:

1. The subject and event are correctly identified.
2. The source is accessible or preserved.
3. The date and record locator are present.
4. The claim accurately represents the source.
5. Allegation/finding status is precise.
6. Conflicting evidence is disclosed.
7. AI analysis is labeled.
8. Required human review is complete.
9. The official response is included or solicited when appropriate.
10. Freshness and confidence are visible.
